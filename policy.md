Phase B: University Communications Office Synthetic Media Policy

1. Purpose

This policy governs the creation, use, publication, and distribution of synthetic audio, video, voices, avatars, digital replicas, and other realistic AI-generated media produced by or on behalf of the University Communications Office.

The goal is to allow limited, useful applications of synthetic media while protecting accuracy, informed consent, institutional credibility, individual identity, and public trust.

This policy applies to university employees, student workers, contractors, agencies, consultants, and vendors producing content for the Communications Office.

2. Organizational Context

The University Communications Office produces public-facing and internal communications for students, faculty, staff, families, alumni, prospective students, media organizations, and the general public.

Possible uses of synthetic media in this setting include:

multilingual communication,

accessibility support,

narration,

orientation material,

instructional or informational videos,

low-risk internal training,

clearly fictional or generic presenters.

However, university communications also carry institutional authority. A synthetic message can easily create confusion about whether a president, dean, professor, employee, or student actually made a statement.

For that reason, synthetic media must not be treated as a default production method. It may be used only when there is a clear communication benefit and the risks can be adequately controlled.

3. Definition

For this policy, synthetic media means audio, video, or visual media generated or materially altered using artificial intelligence in a way that creates or changes a realistic voice, person, event, statement, or presentation.

Examples include:

AI-generated voices,

cloned voices,

AI-generated avatars,

digital replicas,

realistic face replacement,

lip synchronization that changes what a person appears to say,

AI-generated video that resembles a real recording.

Minor edits such as color correction, captioning, noise reduction, background cleanup, or standard image enhancement are not treated as synthetic representation unless they materially change what a person appears to say or do.

4. Core Policy Principles

All synthetic-media projects must follow these principles:

Accuracy first — the underlying message must be verified before generation.

Consent first — identifiable people may not be synthetically represented without informed, documented permission.

Disclosure must be visible — realistic synthetic content must be clearly identified as synthetic.

Human accountability remains — AI tools do not replace editorial or institutional responsibility.

Provenance should be preserved — available content credentials and internal records should be maintained.

Detection is only supporting evidence — automated detectors must not be treated as proof.

Higher risk requires stronger review — the more consequential the communication, the stricter the approval process.

Some uses are prohibited regardless of disclosure.

5. Permitted Uses

Synthetic media may be used for the following purposes when all other requirements in this policy are satisfied:

5.1 Generic or Fictional Presenters

The office may use clearly generic or fictional synthetic presenters when they are not designed to impersonate a real identifiable person.

Examples:

orientation videos,

informational explainers,

training materials,

campus-service walkthroughs.

5.2 Accessibility

Synthetic narration may be used to improve accessibility when:

the underlying content has already been approved,

the synthetic nature of the narration is disclosed when appropriate,

no unauthorized real-person voice clone is used.

5.3 Multilingual Versions

Approved university communications may be converted into multilingual synthetic audio or video when:

the original script has been approved,

the translation is reviewed for accuracy,

the synthetic presentation is disclosed.

5.4 Low-Risk Internal Training

Synthetic presenters or narration may be used for internal employee or student-worker training when the content does not involve:

disciplinary decisions,

legal advice,

medical advice,

emergency communications,

high-stakes institutional decisions.

5.5 Authorized Digital Replicas

A real person's digital replica may be used only when:

the person has provided written, informed consent,

the specific use has been approved,

the content remains within the agreed scope,

the artifact is clearly disclosed as synthetic.

5.6 Educational Demonstrations

Synthetic media may be used in AI-literacy, media-literacy, research, or classroom demonstrations when the purpose is clearly educational and the synthetic nature of the content is obvious or explicitly disclosed.

6. Prohibited Uses

The Communications Office must not create or publish synthetic media that:

uses a real person's voice, face, likeness, or other identifiable characteristics without documented consent;

makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not authorize;

creates fake testimonials, interviews, quotations, endorsements, or eyewitness accounts;

impersonates university leadership, faculty, staff, students, public officials, or other identifiable individuals;

creates synthetic emergency, crisis, safety, or law-enforcement announcements;

communicates admissions decisions through a synthetic replica of a human decision-maker;

communicates disciplinary actions through a synthetic replica;

communicates individualized financial-aid decisions through a synthetic replica;

communicates employment decisions through a synthetic replica;

creates synthetic political endorsements or opposition statements using an identifiable person's likeness;

portrays minors using realistic synthetic replicas without appropriate legal review and explicit authorization;

materially alters documentary footage while presenting it as an authentic recording of an event;

intentionally removes or conceals synthetic-media disclosure;

attempts to defeat AI-detection, watermarking, provenance, or content-credential systems;

creates content that a reasonable viewer would likely mistake for an authentic recording of a real person when no such recording occurred.

Disclosure does not make a prohibited use acceptable.

7. Consent Workflow

When a real person's voice, likeness, face, or identifiable characteristics are involved, the following consent process is required.

7.1 Before Production

The project owner must obtain written consent before generating the content.

The consent record must specify:

the person's name,

the type of synthetic representation,

the purpose of the project,

the intended message or category of messages,

the expected audience,

distribution channels,

whether translation or modification is allowed,

whether future reuse is permitted,

the approval period.

7.2 Scope of Consent

Consent must be specific.

Permission for one video does not automatically allow:

future campaigns,

unrelated messages,

different audiences,

different platforms,

new scripts,

political or commercial uses.

7.3 Duration

Consent should normally expire after 12 months unless the agreement specifies a shorter period.

Continued use after expiration requires renewal.

7.4 Revocation

A person may revoke permission for future generation at any time.

After revocation:

no new content may be generated,

scheduled but unpublished content must be reviewed,

existing published content should be evaluated to determine whether removal is required or appropriate.

7.5 Recordkeeping

Consent records must be stored with the project documentation.

8. Disclosure Standards

All realistic public-facing synthetic media must be clearly disclosed.

8.1 Video

Public-facing synthetic video should include:

a visible on-screen disclosure,

disclosure in the accompanying caption or description,

platform AI-content labels when available.

Recommended language:

This content includes an AI-generated voice, presenter, or other synthetic media.

If a real person's authorized digital replica is used:

This message uses an AI-generated representation created with the individual's authorization.

8.2 Audio

Synthetic audio should include:

spoken disclosure when practical,

written disclosure wherever the audio is posted.

8.3 Persistent Disclosure

Important disclosure should be embedded within the artifact itself whenever possible.

A description-only label is insufficient for realistic synthetic content because descriptions can be separated from downloaded or reposted media.

8.4 No Misleading Placement

Disclosure must not be:

hidden in fine print,

placed only at the end of a long video,

omitted from short-form excerpts,

written in language a typical viewer is unlikely to understand.

9. Provenance Requirements

The Communications Office must maintain provenance and production records for each synthetic-media project.

The record should include:

approved final script,

original source material,

generation tool used,

date of generation,

creator or vendor,

consent documentation when applicable,

reviewer names,

approval records,

final published version,

publication channels,

materially different versions.

Where available, C2PA Content Credentials or equivalent provenance mechanisms should be enabled and preserved.

Machine-readable provenance does not replace visible disclosure.

If metadata is lost during platform processing, the university remains responsible for maintaining its own internal records.

10. Accuracy Verification

The underlying message must be verified before synthetic generation.

The responsible employee must verify, as applicable:

names,

dates,

statistics,

quotations,

deadlines,

policy statements,

procedures,

contact information,

institutional claims.

Synthetic-media tools must not be treated as authoritative sources for factual content.

If the approved script changes materially after review, the revised version must be reviewed again.

11. Review and Approval

11.1 Standard-Risk Projects

Low-risk synthetic projects require approval from:

the project owner, and

the Communications Office reviewer responsible for the relevant channel.

11.2 Higher-Risk Projects

Projects involving any of the following require review by the Communications Director or designated senior reviewer:

a real person's digital replica,

a recognizable cloned voice,

institutional leadership,

sensitive personal information,

reputational risk,

public controversy,

large-scale automated distribution.

11.3 Legal, Privacy, or Risk Review

Additional review is required when the project involves:

minors,

privacy-sensitive information,

contractual rights,

high-stakes institutional statements,

uncertain consent,

possible legal or regulatory exposure.

11.4 Review Questions

Before approval, reviewers must ask:

Why does this communication need to be synthetic?

Has the underlying message been verified?

Does every identifiable person consent?

Could a reasonable viewer misunderstand who actually spoke?

Is the disclosure clear and persistent?

Could the artifact become misleading if clipped or reposted?

Are provenance records maintained?

Is there a lower-risk non-synthetic alternative?

12. Refusal Standard

The Communications Office must refuse a proposed synthetic-media project when the communication benefit does not justify the risk of deception, impersonation, confusion, or reputational harm.

A project should be refused when:

consent is unclear,

disclosure cannot be made clear,

the audience is unlikely to understand the synthetic nature of the content,

the communication involves an emergency or high-stakes decision,

the project creates unnecessary ambiguity about who is speaking,

the office cannot preserve appropriate records,

the intended use falls outside the original consent,

a conventional human recording can achieve the same purpose with materially lower risk.

Efficiency, convenience, or cost savings alone are not sufficient reasons to approve a high-risk synthetic representation.

13. Detection Policy

Synthetic-media detectors may be used during:

quality review,

authenticity investigations,

incident response,

vendor evaluation.

A detector result must not be treated as proof that media is authentic or synthetic.

Detection should be evaluated alongside:

source files,

provenance information,

internal records,

publication history,

consent documentation,

direct confirmation from the represented person or office.

This requirement reflects the Task 6 finding that detection confidence can vary across media types.

14. Scale and Automation Controls

When synthetic media is generated at scale, the office must review the workflow rather than relying only on final-output review.

Controls should include:

approved templates,

restricted data inputs,

access permissions,

approved scripts,

sample review before mass generation,

automated logging,

version control,

rollback procedures.

A change to a template or data source that could materially affect thousands of outputs requires renewed review.

15. Vendor Requirements

External vendors producing synthetic media for the university must comply with this policy.

Contracts should address:

permitted use of university voice or likeness data,

storage and deletion,

model-training restrictions,

access controls,

breach notification,

consent requirements,

provenance support,

reuse restrictions.

Vendors must not use university-provided voice, image, or likeness data to train unrelated models without explicit written authorization.

16. Incident Response

An incident includes:

unauthorized synthetic representation,

removed or misleading disclosure,

incorrect synthetic communication,

compromised digital-replica data,

unauthorized reuse,

misleading redistribution,

publication outside the approved scope.

When an incident occurs:

preserve the artifact, URLs, screenshots, and relevant records;

stop further university-controlled publication when appropriate;

notify the Communications Director;

notify Legal, Privacy, Information Security, or leadership when necessary;

contact the hosting platform if removal or correction is required;

notify the represented person when appropriate;

publish a correction or authenticity notice if audiences may have been misled;

revoke access to compromised source materials or digital replicas when possible;

document the incident and corrective actions;

review the underlying workflow before additional content is produced.

17. Training

Employees and contractors who produce synthetic media should receive training on:

consent,

disclosure,

provenance,

authenticity risks,

privacy,

incident response,

platform requirements,

limits of automated detection.

Training should be refreshed when major policy or technology changes occur.

18. Policy Review

This policy should be reviewed at least annually and after any significant synthetic-media incident.

Review should consider changes in:

generation technology,

detection capability,

provenance standards,

university practices,

law and regulation,

platform policies,

professional norms.

19. Limitations and Residual Risk

This policy reduces risk but cannot guarantee that synthetic university content will never be misunderstood, altered, or misused.

19.1 Disclosure Can Be Removed

A person can crop a watermark, remove introductory language, extract audio, screen-record a video, or share only a short excerpt.

Embedding disclosure helps, but it cannot guarantee that disclosure will survive every transformation.

19.2 Provenance Can Be Lost

Content Credentials and metadata depend on compatible tools and platforms.

A screen recording or unsupported transformation may separate the artifact from its provenance information.

Provenance therefore supports authenticity verification but cannot replace other safeguards.

19.3 Detection Is Imperfect

My Task 6 work showed that detector confidence can differ across media types.

A strong synthetic-audio result did not mean that visual classification was equally decisive.

Detection should therefore remain supporting evidence rather than an authentication guarantee.

19.4 Consent Can Still Be Misunderstood

Even written consent may not capture every future consequence of a digital replica.

A person may understand the immediate project but not anticipate how audiences will react or how the artifact may later be redistributed.

Narrow, project-specific consent reduces this risk but does not eliminate it.

19.5 Good-Faith Compliance Is Required

An employee may:

misunderstand a boundary,

accidentally omit a disclosure,

reuse an approved asset outside its original scope,

fail to update a record,

underestimate audience confusion.

Training, review, and documentation reduce these risks but cannot remove human error.

19.6 Bad-Faith Actors Are Not Controlled by Internal Policy

An internal university policy governs authorized university activity.

It cannot prevent an outsider from:

cloning a university employee's voice,

removing labels,

fabricating a university announcement,

reposting content deceptively,

using external tools to create unauthorized synthetic media.

The university can respond to these incidents, but it cannot guarantee prevention.

19.7 Platform Enforcement Is Outside University Control

The university can follow platform rules and report violations, but it cannot control:

platform detection accuracy,

moderation speed,

label persistence,

third-party reposting,

private distribution channels.

19.8 Trust Can Still Be Damaged

Even responsible synthetic-media use can contribute to uncertainty about whether future university communications are genuine.

For this reason, synthetic media should not become the default communication method.

The strongest final safeguard is restraint:

If synthetic media does not provide a meaningful communication benefit, the University Communications Office should use ordinary human-created communication instead.
