Phase A: Ethical Analysis of Synthetic Representation

1. Reflection on My Task 6 Artifact

In Task 6, I worked directly with synthetic-media tools by creating AI-generated audio and video from a narrative that I had already verified in an earlier task. I used ElevenLabs for synthetic audio and HeyGen for synthetic video, and I also evaluated the outputs using Hive's AI-content detection tools. The artifact was created for an academic purpose, the information in it was truthful, and the synthetic nature of the content was disclosed.

Looking back at the artifact after completing the creation process, what stands out most to me is how easily a message can be separated from the person who appears to deliver it. During Task 6, I was mainly focused on whether the tools could generate a convincing result and how accurately detection tools could identify it. In Task 7, I am looking at the same artifact differently. Even when the message itself is accurate, synthetic media can still create uncertainty about who actually spoke, who is responsible for the message, and how much trust a viewer should place in the voice or face presenting it.

Another thing I noticed is that the technical process itself can feel normal and harmless when the intent is legitimate. I entered a script, generated audio or video, reviewed the output, and tested it. However, the same workflow could be used with a false script, another person's likeness, or without disclosure. This made me realize that the ethical risk does not come only from the tool. It also depends on the truthfulness of the message, whether the represented person consented, how the artifact is presented, and how widely it can be distributed.

My Task 6 detection work also influenced this reflection. The synthetic audio was detected with high confidence, while the visual detection was less decisive. This showed me that detection is useful, but it is not a perfect safeguard. A viewer or organization cannot assume that every synthetic artifact will always be detected accurately.

The most important lesson I take from Task 6 is that responsible synthetic-media use requires more than simply creating truthful content. It also requires transparency, consent, accountability, and consideration of what could happen if the artifact is copied, edited, or removed from its original context.

There are also uses I would not be comfortable creating. I would not create synthetic content that makes a real person appear to say something they did not authorize, even if the purpose seemed harmless. I would also avoid creating realistic synthetic content without clear disclosure because viewers could reasonably interpret it as an authentic recording.

2. Thought Experiments Across Four Ethical Axes

The purpose of these thought experiments is to start with the same basic capability I used in Task 6 and change one important condition at a time. This helps identify exactly where the ethical risk changes.

2.1 Truth Axis — Same Technology, False Message

Scenario

In Task 6, the narrative I used was based on information I had already verified. Now imagine using the same ElevenLabs and HeyGen workflow to create a video containing false information.

For example, suppose someone creates a realistic synthetic video that appears to be an official university announcement. The video states that classes have been canceled because of a serious safety issue, even though no such event has occurred. The synthetic presenter speaks confidently, the video looks professional, and the clip is shared through student group chats.

Students could begin leaving campus, contacting family members, or sharing the warning with others before the university has time to correct it.

Ethical Analysis

The technology itself has not changed. The major difference is that the synthetic delivery mechanism is now being used to give false information the appearance of an authoritative announcement.

The harm comes from combining fabrication with a realistic presentation style. A plain false text message may already cause confusion, but synthetic audio or video can make the claim seem more credible because people are accustomed to treating a visible or audible speaker as evidence that someone actually made the statement.

This scenario shows why truth verification should happen before synthetic content is generated. Disclosure that a video is AI-generated would help, but it would not make intentionally false information ethically acceptable.

2.2 Consent Axis — Using Someone Else's Voice or Likeness

Scenario

Instead of using an authorized or generic synthetic representation, imagine that I take publicly available recordings of a university professor and use them to clone the professor's voice without permission.

I then create an audio message in which the professor appears to announce a change in grading policy. The grading change might even be real, but the professor never recorded or approved the message.

Students hear a voice that sounds like their professor and reasonably assume that the professor personally made the announcement.

Ethical Analysis

The important change here is consent.

Even if the information is accurate, the synthetic recording falsely represents the professor as personally making a statement. The professor loses control over how their identity and reputation are being used.

This is different from simply using an AI narrator because a recognizable human voice carries personal credibility and accountability. The audience may react differently because they believe a specific person is speaking.

This thought experiment shows that consent should not be treated as optional simply because the underlying message is truthful. If a real person's voice, face, or likeness is used, permission should be specific to the intended use rather than assumed from the fact that recordings of the person already exist publicly.

2.3 Context Axis — Disclosure Is Removed

Scenario

In Task 6, the synthetic nature of the artifact was disclosed. Imagine that the original video contains a clear AI-generated-content label.

Someone downloads the video, crops the frame so that the disclosure is no longer visible, and uploads a short excerpt to another social-media platform. Another user then shares the clip with a caption suggesting that it is a real recording.

The original creator may have acted responsibly, but people viewing the new version no longer see the disclosure.

Ethical Analysis

This scenario changes the context rather than the original content.

It shows that disclosure works only while the disclosure remains connected to the artifact. Once content is downloaded, edited, screen-recorded, clipped, or reposted, information about how it was created may disappear.

This creates an important governance problem. A creator can behave responsibly at the point of publication but still lose control over how the artifact is interpreted later.

Because of this, responsible disclosure should not depend on only one label in a description or at the beginning of a video. Visible disclosure inside the media, accompanying text, and provenance information can work together, although none can guarantee that the context will always survive redistribution.

2.4 Scale Axis — From One Artifact to Thousands

Scenario

For Task 6, I created and evaluated a small number of synthetic artifacts manually. Now imagine connecting the same type of generation tools to an automated workflow that can produce thousands of personalized videos.

A university could potentially use this system to create customized orientation videos for each incoming student. The system could automatically insert each student's name, program, deadlines, and other information.

That could be useful, but imagine that the underlying data contains an error or that an unapproved message is entered into the template. Instead of affecting one video, the mistake could immediately appear in thousands of personalized messages.

The same capability could also be intentionally misused to create thousands of misleading messages targeted toward different groups.

Ethical Analysis

Scale changes both the potential benefit and the potential harm.

Synthetic-media systems can make useful communication faster and more accessible, but automation also reduces the time and effort required to reproduce mistakes, misinformation, or impersonation.

At small scale, a person may be able to manually review every artifact. At large scale, that becomes unrealistic. Governance therefore has to move beyond reviewing individual videos and instead control the underlying templates, approved scripts, data sources, access permissions, and generation workflow.

This thought experiment shows that scale is not simply a productivity question. It changes the risk because one poor decision can be reproduced almost instantly across a large audience.

3. Survey of the Mitigation Landscape

The four thought experiments show that synthetic-media risk cannot be addressed by one safeguard. Different mitigations address different parts of the problem: disclosure helps audiences understand what they are seeing, provenance provides information about origin and editing history, detection attempts to identify synthetic signals, laws create enforceable boundaries, platforms control distribution, and professional norms establish expectations for responsible behavior.

The important question for each mitigation is not only what it promises, but also where it can fail.

3.1 Disclosure and Labeling

What it promises

Disclosure is one of the simplest ways to reduce deception. A visible label, spoken acknowledgment, watermark, or platform notice can tell viewers that audio or video was generated or meaningfully altered using AI.

This approach is already reflected in major platform rules. YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic content that has been meaningfully generated or altered with AI, including content that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not do.[1] TikTok also provides creator labels and can automatically apply an AI-generated label when it detects certain signals, including Content Credentials.[2] Meta uses "AI info" labels based on technical signals and creator disclosure.[3]

Where it breaks

Disclosure depends on the label remaining connected to the content. A video can be cropped, screen-recorded, clipped, or downloaded and reposted without its original description. A spoken disclosure at the beginning can disappear when someone shares only the middle of a clip.

There is also a human limitation. A label can tell a viewer that AI was involved, but it does not guarantee that the viewer understands what was changed or how important the change was.

This connects directly to my context-axis thought experiment. My original artifact can be responsibly labeled, but I cannot guarantee that the disclosure will remain visible after redistribution.

My conclusion: disclosure should be required for realistic synthetic representation, but it should be treated as one layer of protection rather than proof that an artifact will never be misunderstood.

3.2 Provenance and Content Credentials

What it promises

Provenance approaches try to answer a different question from detection. Instead of asking, "Does this file look AI-generated?" provenance asks, "What information can be verified about where this file came from and what happened to it?"

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) develops technical standards that allow creators and tools to attach verifiable information about an asset's origin and editing history. Importantly, C2PA itself states that provenance should verify whether assertions are associated with an asset and have not been tampered with; it does not judge whether the content is truthful or "good."[4]

This distinction is useful. A synthetic university video could carry verifiable information showing which organization created it, what tool was used, and whether it was modified after creation.

Where it breaks

Provenance is not the same as truth. A person could cryptographically sign a false statement. The signature would help verify where the file came from, but it would not make the statement accurate.

Provenance also depends on tools and platforms preserving and displaying the information. If content is screen-recorded, transformed, or moved through systems that do not preserve credentials, the viewer may no longer receive the original provenance signal.

In Task 6, my strongest direct evidence came from detection results rather than from demonstrating that provenance metadata survives every possible transformation. Therefore, I should not claim from my own experiment that provenance solves the redistribution problem.

My conclusion: provenance is valuable for chain-of-custody and origin verification, but it should support visible disclosure and organizational records rather than replace them.

3.3 Automated Detection

What it promises

Detection tools attempt to identify whether audio, images, or video contain signals associated with AI generation or manipulation. They can be useful when an artifact arrives without reliable metadata or disclosure.

I tested this directly in Task 6 using Hive. The synthetic audio generated with ElevenLabs was identified as AI-generated with very high confidence. The visual result from the HeyGen artifact was less decisive in at least one sampled frame, while its synthetic audio was again detected with high confidence.

This was one of the most important practical findings from Task 6 because it showed that detection performance can differ across modalities.

Where it breaks

A detector produces an assessment, not absolute proof. Different generators, media types, compression levels, edits, and re-recording methods can affect the signals available to a detector.

Detection also creates an ongoing technical problem: generators continue improving while detectors attempt to recognize new generation methods. A result from one detector on one artifact therefore should not be generalized into a claim that all synthetic media can reliably be identified.

There is also a risk in the opposite direction. If an organization treats a low AI score as proof that something is authentic, a false negative could create misplaced confidence.

My conclusion: detection is useful as a supporting forensic signal, but no important authenticity decision should depend on a detector alone.

3.4 Legal and Regulatory Approaches

What they promise

Laws can establish enforceable boundaries around particular synthetic-media harms.

The European Union's AI Act provides one example of a transparency-based approach. Article 50 transparency obligations began applying on August 2, 2026. Among other requirements, the framework addresses machine-readable marking of certain AI-generated or manipulated content and disclosure when people are exposed to deepfakes.[5]

The United States also has laws aimed at specific harms rather than all synthetic media. The federal TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed in 2025, covers non-consensual intimate imagery including certain digital forgeries. The Federal Trade Commission began enforcing the platform notice-and-removal requirements in May 2026; covered platforms must provide a removal process and act on valid requests within 48 hours.[6]

These examples show two different regulatory approaches: one emphasizes transparency around AI-generated content, while another targets a specific category of non-consensual harm.

Where they break

Law does not provide one universal rule for every synthetic-media situation. Requirements differ by jurisdiction, type of content, audience, and harm.

Law is also often reactive. A victim may already have experienced impersonation, reputational damage, or unwanted distribution before a legal or platform remedy becomes useful.

Finally, something can be technically legal while still being inappropriate for an organization. A university, newsroom, or company may need a stricter internal standard than the minimum required by law.

My conclusion: legal compliance should be a minimum boundary for organizational behavior, not the complete definition of responsible use.

3.5 Platform Policies

What they promise

Platforms can intervene at the point where synthetic content reaches large audiences.

YouTube requires disclosure of realistic altered or synthetic content and displays information to viewers about how certain content was made.[1] TikTok asks creators to identify AI-generated content and can automatically apply labels using signals such as C2PA Content Credentials.[2] Meta applies AI-related labels based on technical indicators or user disclosure and has continued expanding its transparency approach.[3]

Platforms therefore provide an important distribution-level layer that individual creators cannot reproduce on their own.

Where they break

Platform rules still depend on successful detection, technical signals, creator compliance, and enforcement. A platform can also govern only the content distributed through its own service.

A synthetic artifact can move from one platform to another, be downloaded into a private messaging group, or be edited before reposting. The new copy may no longer carry the same disclosure or metadata.

Different platforms may also define reportable or label-worthy synthetic content differently, which can create inconsistent audience expectations.

My conclusion: organizations should follow platform policies, but they should never outsource their ethical responsibility to the platform that hosts the content.

3.6 Professional and Organizational Norms

Different professions treat synthetic media differently because the consequences of misrepresentation are different.

Journalism

The Associated Press updated its AI newsroom standards in July 2026. Its approach allows AI assistance for selected tasks but keeps editorial judgment, verification, accuracy, and accountability with journalists. AP also continues to prohibit generative AI from creating, altering, or enhancing news photography.[7]

This is important because journalism depends heavily on evidence and public trust.

Where it breaks: professional rules apply primarily to people and organizations that choose to follow them. They cannot stop outsiders from producing material that imitates legitimate journalism.

Entertainment

SAG-AFTRA's digital-replica protections emphasize informed consent. Its guidance says performers should receive a reasonably specific description of the intended use of a digital replica and generally rejects blanket consent across unrelated projects.[8]

This shows that consent should not simply mean "the person agreed to AI once." The intended use matters.

Where it breaks: consent agreements can govern authorized productions, but they cannot technically prevent someone outside the agreement from attempting unauthorized cloning.

Advertising and Marketing

Advertising organizations have increasingly focused on disclosure when AI materially affects authenticity or representation. The Association of National Advertisers states that consumers should receive clear or accessible notice when failure to disclose GenAI use in audio or visual advertising would materially mislead a reasonable consumer.[9] In August 2026, the Interactive Advertising Bureau released an updated risk-based transparency framework covering synthetic voices, digital twins, AI-generated video, and other consumer-facing uses.[10]

Where it breaks: a materiality-based system still requires human judgment about what might mislead a reasonable consumer. Different organizations may make that judgment differently.

Education

Higher-education guidance increasingly emphasizes transparency and continued human responsibility. A 2026 EDUCAUSE framework recommends disclosure when AI-generated material becomes a visible part of the learning experience and states that humans remain responsible for accuracy, appropriateness, and alignment with learning outcomes.[11] UNESCO similarly advocates a human-centered approach to GenAI in education and research.[12]

Where it breaks: universities have many audiences and decentralized workflows. A policy may exist institutionally while individual instructors, departments, students, or vendors interpret AI use differently.

Political Consulting

The American Association of Political Consultants takes a stricter position on deceptive synthetic representation. Its policy treats deceptive "deep fake" generative-AI content as incompatible with its professional code of ethics and states that adding a disclaimer is not enough to make such deceptive content acceptable.[13]

This is an important contrast with a simple disclosure model. Some uses are considered inappropriate even when they could technically be labeled.

Where it breaks: professional association rules apply to members and ethical participants. A bad-faith actor does not become constrained simply because a professional organization condemns the behavior.

3.7 Overall Assessment of the Mitigation Landscape

The most important conclusion from this survey is that none of these mitigations solves the synthetic-media problem alone.

Disclosure can be removed.

Provenance can verify origin information but not truth.

Detection can be uncertain.

Law targets specific harms and jurisdictions.

Platform policies depend on enforcement and technical signals.

Professional standards depend on participants agreeing to follow them.

However, this does not make the mitigations useless. Their value comes from using them together.

A responsibly created synthetic artifact can begin with verified information and informed consent, contain visible disclosure, preserve provenance information, pass through human review, comply with platform and legal requirements, and maintain internal records. Detection can then serve as an additional verification tool if questions arise later.

The goal is therefore not to create one perfect safeguard. It is to create enough independent layers that the failure of one does not automatically remove every protection.

4. Overall Ethical Responsibility

After reflecting on Task 6, writing the four thought experiments, and reviewing the mitigation landscape, I do not think the main ethical problem is simply the existence of synthetic-media technology.

The more important issue is the separation of representation from accountability.

A realistic voice or face normally carries an assumption that a person actually spoke, appeared, or participated in creating the message. Synthetic media weakens that assumption. Once a voice, face, or realistic presenter can be generated independently of the person apparently delivering the message, audiences need additional ways to understand who actually created and authorized the communication.

The four axes helped make this clearer:

The truth axis showed that realistic delivery can increase the credibility of false information.

The consent axis showed that even truthful information can become unethical when another person's identity is used without authorization.

The context axis showed that the creator can lose control of disclosure after redistribution.

The scale axis showed that automation can multiply both legitimate benefits and harmful mistakes.

If I had to place primary responsibility somewhere, I would place it first on the person or organization producing and publishing the synthetic artifact.

The producer chooses the script, the voice or likeness, the generation tool, the disclosure method, and the original distribution channel. That makes the producer the actor with the earliest opportunity to prevent harm.

Platforms also have responsibility because they control distribution and can provide labels, reporting mechanisms, and enforcement. Regulators establish enforceable boundaries for serious harms. Audiences also benefit from media literacy and healthy skepticism.

However, I do not think responsibility should be shifted primarily onto viewers. A viewer should not be expected to perform forensic analysis every time they encounter a realistic video or voice recording.

I also do not think the fact that every mitigation can be defeated makes mitigation pointless. Security and governance often work through layers rather than guarantees. Disclosure may help an ordinary viewer even if a determined person can crop it. Provenance may help establish origin even if it cannot prove truth. Detection may provide useful evidence even if it is imperfect.

The realistic goal is therefore risk reduction and accountability, not perfect prevention.

There are also uses I would personally refuse regardless of whether disclosure could technically make them compliant. I would not create a realistic synthetic representation that makes an identifiable person appear to give a statement they did not authorize. I would also refuse synthetic emergency communication or other high-stakes messaging when a reasonable audience could confuse the artificial representation with a real human statement.

That refusal follows from the same lesson I learned from Task 6: once representation becomes easy to generate, the producer has to take greater responsibility for preserving the connection between the message, its actual source, and the audience's understanding of it.

5. Implications for Governance

The Phase A analysis leads to several principles that should guide the policy developed in Phase B.

5.1 Verify the Message Before Generating It

Synthetic presentation should never be used to bypass ordinary fact-checking or approval. The underlying script should be verified before the voice, avatar, or video is generated.

5.2 Require Specific Consent for Real People

If an identifiable person's face, voice, likeness, or recognizable characteristics are used, consent should be informed, documented, and specific to the intended use. Public availability of recordings should not be treated as permission to clone a person.

5.3 Make Disclosure Persistent and Redundant

Realistic synthetic media should be clearly disclosed. Important disclosure should exist inside the artifact when possible, not only in a description that can easily become separated from the file.

5.4 Preserve Provenance and Internal Records

Where tools support Content Credentials or similar provenance mechanisms, they should be preserved. Organizations should also maintain their own source files, approved scripts, consent records, generation details, and publication records.

5.5 Do Not Treat Detection as Proof

Detection tools can support review or investigation, but a detector result should never be the sole basis for deciding whether important media is authentic.

5.6 Apply Human Review Before Publication

A human reviewer should evaluate accuracy, consent, disclosure, audience risk, and potential for misunderstanding before realistic synthetic content is published.

5.7 Apply Stronger Controls as Scale Increases

An automated workflow that can generate thousands of artifacts requires controls over templates, data inputs, permissions, and approval processes. Reviewing only the final outputs is not enough.

5.8 Plan for Redistribution and Misuse

The organization should assume that content may be downloaded, clipped, reposted, or separated from its original context. Incident-response procedures should exist before publication rather than being invented only after misuse occurs.

5.9 Follow Law and Platform Rules, but Set a Higher Internal Standard

Legal compliance and platform disclosure requirements establish important minimums. They should not prevent an organization from refusing a use that presents unnecessary ethical or reputational risk.

5.10 Preserve the Ability to Say No

The final policy should contain prohibited uses and a refusal standard. Disclosure should not automatically make every use acceptable.

This is the most important governance implication of my Phase A analysis. Responsible synthetic-media governance is not only about determining how to create synthetic media safely. It also requires determining when the organization should not create it at all.
