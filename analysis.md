Phase A: Ethical Analysis of Synthetic Representation

1. Reflection on My Task 6 Artifact

In Task 6, I worked directly with synthetic-media tools by creating AI-generated audio and video from a narrative that I had already verified in an earlier task. I used ElevenLabs for synthetic audio and HeyGen for synthetic video, and I also evaluated the outputs using Hive's AI-content detection tools. The artifact was created for an academic purpose, the information in it was truthful, and the synthetic nature of the content was disclosed.

Looking back at the artifact after completing the creation process, what stands out most to me is how easily a message can be separated from the person who appears to deliver it. During Task 6, I was mainly focused on whether the tools could generate a convincing result and how accurately detection tools could identify it. In Task 7, I am looking at the same artifact differently. Even when the message itself is accurate, synthetic media can still create uncertainty about who actually spoke, who is responsible for the message, and how much trust a viewer should place in the voice or face presenting it.

Another thing I noticed is that the technical process itself can feel normal and harmless when the intent is legitimate. I entered a script, generated audio or video, reviewed the output, and tested it. However, the same workflow could be used with a false script, another person's likeness, or without disclosure. This made me realize that the ethical risk does not come only from the tool. It also depends on the truthfulness of the message, whether the represented person consented, how the artifact is presented, and how widely it can be distributed.

My Task 6 detection work also influenced this reflection. The synthetic audio was detected with high confidence, while the visual detection was less decisive. This showed me that detection is useful, but it is not a perfect safeguard. A viewer or organization cannot assume that every synthetic artifact will always be detected accurately.

The most important lesson I take from Task 6 is that responsible synthetic-media use requires more than simply creating truthful content. It also requires transparency, consent, accountability, and consideration of what could happen if the artifact is copied, edited, or removed from its original context.

There are also uses I would not be comfortable creating. I would not create synthetic content that makes a real person appear to say something they did not authorize, even if the purpose seemed harmless. I would also avoid creating realistic synthetic content without clear disclosure because viewers could reasonably interpret it as an authentic recording.

2. Thought Experiments Across Four Ethical Axes

The purpose of these thought experiments is to start with the same basic capability I used in Task 6 and change one important condition at a time. This helps identify exactly where the ethical risk changes.

2.1 Truth Axis — Same Technology, False Message

Scenario

In Task 6, the narrative I used was based on information I had already verified. Now imagine using the same ElevenLabs and HeyGen workflow to create a video containing false information.

For example, suppose someone creates a realistic synthetic video that appears to be an official university announcement. The video states that classes have been canceled because of a serious safety issue, even though no such event has occurred. The synthetic presenter speaks confidently, the video looks professional, and the clip is shared through student group chats.

Students could begin leaving campus, contacting family members, or sharing the warning with others before the university has time to correct it.

Ethical Analysis

The technology itself has not changed. The major difference is that the synthetic delivery mechanism is now being used to give false information the appearance of an authoritative announcement.

The harm comes from combining fabrication with a realistic presentation style. A plain false text message may already cause confusion, but synthetic audio or video can make the claim seem more credible because people are accustomed to treating a visible or audible speaker as evidence that someone actually made the statement.

This scenario shows why truth verification should happen before synthetic content is generated. Disclosure that a video is AI-generated would help, but it would not make intentionally false information ethically acceptable.

2.2 Consent Axis — Using Someone Else's Voice or Likeness

Scenario

Instead of using an authorized or generic synthetic representation, imagine that I take publicly available recordings of a university professor and use them to clone the professor's voice without permission.

I then create an audio message in which the professor appears to announce a change in grading policy. The grading change might even be real, but the professor never recorded or approved the message.

Students hear a voice that sounds like their professor and reasonably assume that the professor personally made the announcement.

Ethical Analysis

The important change here is consent.

Even if the information is accurate, the synthetic recording falsely represents the professor as personally making a statement. The professor loses control over how their identity and reputation are being used.

This is different from simply using an AI narrator because a recognizable human voice carries personal credibility and accountability. The audience may react differently because they believe a specific person is speaking.

This thought experiment shows that consent should not be treated as optional simply because the underlying message is truthful. If a real person's voice, face, or likeness is used, permission should be specific to the intended use rather than assumed from the fact that recordings of the person already exist publicly.

2.3 Context Axis — Disclosure Is Removed

Scenario

In Task 6, the synthetic nature of the artifact was disclosed. Imagine that the original video contains a clear AI-generated-content label.

Someone downloads the video, crops the frame so that the disclosure is no longer visible, and uploads a short excerpt to another social-media platform. Another user then shares the clip with a caption suggesting that it is a real recording.

The original creator may have acted responsibly, but people viewing the new version no longer see the disclosure.

Ethical Analysis

This scenario changes the context rather than the original content.

It shows that disclosure works only while the disclosure remains connected to the artifact. Once content is downloaded, edited, screen-recorded, clipped, or reposted, information about how it was created may disappear.

This creates an important governance problem. A creator can behave responsibly at the point of publication but still lose control over how the artifact is interpreted later.

Because of this, responsible disclosure should not depend on only one label in a description or at the beginning of a video. Visible disclosure inside the media, accompanying text, and provenance information can work together, although none can guarantee that the context will always survive redistribution.

2.4 Scale Axis — From One Artifact to Thousands

Scenario

For Task 6, I created and evaluated a small number of synthetic artifacts manually. Now imagine connecting the same type of generation tools to an automated workflow that can produce thousands of personalized videos.

A university could potentially use this system to create customized orientation videos for each incoming student. The system could automatically insert each student's name, program, deadlines, and other information.

That could be useful, but imagine that the underlying data contains an error or that an unapproved message is entered into the template. Instead of affecting one video, the mistake could immediately appear in thousands of personalized messages.

The same capability could also be intentionally misused to create thousands of misleading messages targeted toward different groups.

Ethical Analysis

Scale changes both the potential benefit and the potential harm.

Synthetic-media systems can make useful communication faster and more accessible, but automation also reduces the time and effort required to reproduce mistakes, misinformation, or impersonation.

At small scale, a person may be able to manually review every artifact. At large scale, that becomes unrealistic. Governance therefore has to move beyond reviewing individual videos and instead control the underlying templates, approved scripts, data sources, access permissions, and generation workflow.

This thought experiment shows that scale is not simply a productivity question. It changes the risk because one poor decision can be reproduced almost instantly across a large audience.

3. Initial Takeaways From the Four Thought Experiments

The four scenarios show that the ethical risk of synthetic media changes depending on more than whether AI was used.

The truth axis shows that realistic synthetic delivery can increase the credibility of fabricated information.

The consent axis shows that even truthful content can be unethical when another person's identity is used without permission.

The context axis shows that responsible disclosure can disappear when content is edited or redistributed.

The scale axis shows that automation can multiply both useful communication and harmful mistakes.

The main conclusion I draw from these scenarios is that responsible synthetic-media use requires several safeguards at the same time. Truthfulness alone is not enough. Disclosure alone is not enough. Responsible use also requires consent, appropriate context, review, and controls over how the capability is scaled.
